Nervous System

Body stuck in
fight or flight mode

22 July 2026 · 8 min read

← All posts

When your body is stuck in fight or flight mode, the experience does not always feel like fear. More often it feels like an inability to fully switch off — a background alertness, a tension that sleep does not relieve, a sense that rest is always slightly out of reach.

This guide explains what is actually happening physiologically when the body gets stuck in this state — and gives you a concrete, practical path out.

Your body is not malfunctioning. It has adapted to its environment. A body that has been under sustained pressure long enough recalibrates its baseline upward — and that recalibration does not undo itself when the pressure temporarily lifts.

What Is Actually Happening in Your Body

The fight or flight response is governed by the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system (ANS). When a threat is detected — real or perceived — the sympathetic system triggers a cascade of physiological changes: elevated heart rate, cortisol release, muscle tension, redirected blood flow, suppressed digestion.

In acute situations, this cascade resolves once the threat passes. The parasympathetic system — the rest-and-digest branch — takes over, cortisol levels decline, and the body returns to baseline.

When the pressure is chronic rather than acute — sustained professional demands, ongoing interpersonal stress, accumulated emotional weight — the system does not get the clear signal that the threat has passed. It stays partially activated. Over time, this elevated state becomes the body's new normal.

Understanding this as a learned state — rather than a permanent condition — is important. A body that has learned to stay in activation can learn to leave it. This is the core premise of polyvagal theory: the nervous system is plastic, and it responds to the signals it consistently receives.

How to Tell If Your Body Is Stuck

The signs that the body has been stuck in activation are different from acute stress symptoms. They are subtler, more persistent, and easier to normalise:

  • A jaw that is clenched even in moments of relative calm
  • Shoulders that are elevated or held forward as a default posture
  • Breathing that is consistently shallow and does not naturally reach the lower lungs
  • Waking between 2am and 4am — the cortisol surge that precedes morning activation arriving too early
  • Feeling most alert after 9pm when you should be winding down
  • Digestive symptoms with no clear food-based cause
  • A persistent sense of bracing — as if something is about to require a response

For a full breakdown of these patterns, see our posts on fight or flight symptoms and the signs your nervous system is stuck in fight-or-flight.

Why Resting Does Not Fix It

The most common response to chronic activation is to try to rest more. Take a weekend off. Book a holiday. Go to bed earlier. And it helps — temporarily. But the body returns to the elevated state quickly because the recalibrated baseline is still in place.

Rest removes you from the demands. It does not change the internal state the body returns to in the absence of demands. For that, you need inputs that actively engage the parasympathetic nervous system — not just the removal of sympathetic triggers.

The Practical Recovery Path

Step 1: Engage the vagal brake daily

The vagus nerve is the primary pathway of the parasympathetic nervous system. Specific techniques consistently activate it and produce measurable parasympathetic responses. Extended exhale breathing — inhaling for four counts, exhaling for six to eight — is the most accessible. Do this for five to ten minutes at consistent times each day, not only when you feel activated.

Step 2: Use the orienting response deliberately

The orienting response — slowly scanning your environment, taking in details of your actual physical space — directly signals safety through the visual system. This is a technique from somatic trauma therapy that works precisely because it engages the body's existing safety signalling pathways.

Step 3: Add physical discharge

The fight or flight response prepares the body for movement. One of the reasons it stays stuck is that the prepared-for movement never happened — the activation was met with stillness rather than action. Gentle, rhythmic movement — walking, slow stretching, shaking — provides a form of discharge that helps the body complete the response cycle.

Step 4: Process the accumulated load

Regulation techniques address the acute state. But if the emotional burden that has been accumulating is not addressed, the baseline will remain elevated. Structured expressive writing and somatic processing practices work on this deeper layer — the somatic accumulation that represents months or years of stored tension.

Step 5: Build consistency, not intensity

The nervous system changes through repetition. Fifteen minutes of daily practice over eight weeks produces more lasting change than a weekend retreat. The 10-minute nervous system reset is a starting point — but the mechanism is what you do every day, not what you do occasionally.

How Long Recovery Takes

For a body that has been in sustained activation for months or years, recovery is measured in weeks and months — not days. Most people practicing daily vagal activation techniques notice meaningful shifts in their resting state within four to six weeks. Significant changes to the baseline often take three to six months of consistent work.

This is not discouraging — it is accurate. Understanding the timeframe prevents the common pattern of trying something for two weeks, not seeing dramatic change, and concluding that it does not work. The work is cumulative. The system is building new regulatory capacity every day.

Frequently Asked Questions
How do you get your body out of fight or flight mode?
Through consistent, specific inputs to the vagus nerve and parasympathetic nervous system: extended exhale breathing, orienting, gentle rhythmic movement, and structured somatic processing. The key word is consistent — the nervous system changes through repetition, not through single interventions.
How long does it take to get out of fight or flight mode?
For acute activation, a physiological sigh or five minutes of extended exhale breathing can produce a measurable shift within minutes. For a body that has been in chronic activation, shifting the resting baseline typically takes four to eight weeks of daily practice.
Can being stuck in fight or flight damage your health?
Chronic sympathetic activation is associated with elevated cardiovascular risk, immune suppression, digestive disruption, metabolic changes, and accelerated cellular aging. The longer the body remains in this state, the more significant the cumulative biological cost.
Is medication needed to get out of fight or flight mode?
Not necessarily. Many people make significant progress through structured self-practice alone. Medication is sometimes appropriate — particularly when anxiety is clinically severe or when other conditions are present — but the nervous system's capacity for regulation can be developed through non-pharmacological means for most people.
